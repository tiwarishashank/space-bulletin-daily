This is Space Bulletin Weekly with Nora. It's Saturday, November twenty-first.

United Launch Alliance has suffered the first outright failure in its history. Spaceflight Now reports a Vulcan rocket carrying forty Amazon Leo internet satellites lifted off from Cape Canaveral on Sunday, November fifteenth, and failed to reach orbit. ULA said the vehicle suffered an anomaly about ten minutes into flight. In an update on Wednesday, the company said preliminary indications are that low booster thrust was caused by an inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to its BE-4 engines, and not by the engines themselves. ULA said the solid rocket boosters performed normally, following nozzle improvements made for this launch. The Federal Aviation Administration will oversee the investigation. It was only the fifth Vulcan flight, the first in nine months, and the first of thirty-eight Vulcan launches Amazon has bought. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman wrote on X, "Artemis, Moon Base, and the nation need ULA. We are in your corner and here to help."

A European and Japanese spacecraft is set to arrive at Mercury today. The European Space Agency says BepiColombo is scheduled to fire its thrusters this morning to be captured into an elliptical orbit around the planet. The two orbiters in the stack, Europe's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan's Mio, are due to separate on December ninth and tenth, making BepiColombo the first mission to fly two spacecraft around Mercury. Science operations are set to begin in April 2027.

There is a follow-up to last week's Starship flight. Space dot com reports Elon Musk said Ship forty-two, the first Starship upper stage ever caught by the launch tower, will not fly again. Musk said it "could probably be reflown with a few weeks of refurbishment," but that SpaceX will leave it for public display because what it has done is historic. SpaceX also launched a classified mission for the U S Space Force, called USSF three-seventeen, from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California on Friday night. Space dot com says it was the one hundred twenty-first Falcon nine launch of the year.

In a major space deal, Finnish satellite company ICEYE has made an all-cash offer for Italian rocket maker Avio. ICEYE said its offer is forty-one euros a share, a premium of twenty-one point three percent over Avio's closing price on November sixteenth. The company says it wants to build a vertically integrated European space and defense company, combining Avio's launch and propulsion business with its own satellites, and plans to invest an additional one billion euros in Italy over five years. The offer still needs regulatory approvals.

European Space Agency member states have picked the agency's next medium-class science mission. ESA says Plasma Observatory will use seven coordinated spacecraft to track how the Sun's energy moves through Earth's magnetic shield, across different scales of distance and time. ESA's director of science, Carole Mundell, said it "will take European leadership in heliophysics into the next decade and beyond."

In Earth observation, ESA released the first thermal images from the Copernicus Sentinel-3C satellite, launched on September fifteenth. Its radiometer measures land and sea surface temperatures and has two channels dedicated to detecting wildfires. ESA says the images show the instrument is working well even before it is fully commissioned. Closer to home, NASA researchers have produced detailed 3D topography maps of all of California, down to the scale of a single tree. NASA says the airborne lidar maps have a pixel size of one square meter, and the team hopes to use them to help assess wildfire risk and manage natural resources.

At the International Space Station, an uncrewed SpaceX cargo Dragon undocked on Friday after a weather delay. NASA says it is due to splash down off the California coast at about one fifty-five this afternoon, Pacific Time, carrying science and cargo.

In startup funding, Ramon dot Space, which builds computers for satellites, raised one hundred twenty-four million dollars in a Series B round led by PSG, with Micron Technology among the new investors. And Via Satellite reports Bengaluru-based Earth observation company SatSure raised a twenty-one million dollar Series B in a mix of equity, debt and secondary sales. India's sovereign space-tech fund led the round with six point four million dollars. SatSure plans to fly its optical payloads in orbit in 2027.

Finally, SpaceNews reports Chinese launch company Landspace has hot-fire tested the nine engines from a recovered Zhuque-3 first stage and qualified them for reuse, a step toward a reusable rocket.
