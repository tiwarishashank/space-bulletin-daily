This is Space Bulletin Weekly with Nora. It's Saturday, November fourteenth.

SpaceX has caught a Starship upper stage for the first time. Spaceflight Now reports Flight Fifteen lifted off from Starbase, Texas, on Friday afternoon, in a bid to recover both stages with the launch tower's mechanical arms. SpaceX said the Super Heavy booster's boostback burn ended early because of low fuel. Its guidance system then aborted the catch and sent the booster to an offshore landing in the Gulf of Mexico. After a landing burn over the water, its flight termination system was triggered, and the booster exploded. The Ship upper stage reached orbit and deployed twenty-six Starlink version three satellites. On Saturday afternoon, about twenty-two and a half hours after launch, Ship forty-two returned to Starbase and was caught by the tower's arms. Spaceflight Now said the catch took place at three-twenty, Central Time, and called it the first time the upper stage of an orbital launch vehicle has returned to Earth.

NASA has rolled out a long list of contracts for its planned Moon Base. The agency says it selected eight companies for the next phase of its Commercial Lunar Payload Services program: Blue Origin, Firefly Aerospace, Intuitive Machines, ispace U S, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, SpaceX and Voyager Technologies. The ten-year contract has a combined maximum value of ten billion dollars. NASA is also moving ahead with PROMISE, a six-wheeled, nuclear-powered rover built from spare parts and test models for the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers. It is meant to drill about a meter below the surface at the lunar South Pole to look for ice. Blue Origin won a ninety-four million dollar base contract to deliver it, and NASA is targeting launch in late 2028. Administrator Jared Isaacman said, "We are building the Moon Base mission by mission." Separately, NASA picked SpaceX to launch communications satellites to cislunar space aboard a Starship in 2028.

In Europe, the Euclid space telescope has released its first major batch of science data. The European Space Agency says the release, called DR1 Foundation, covers about thirteen months of observations and captures the light of more than a billion objects. It includes more than nine hundred million galaxies, around fifteen thousand strong gravitational lensing candidates and more than sixty million quasar candidates. ESA's director of science, Carole Mundell, said, "These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos."

In Earth observation, France has ordered its next generation of spy satellites. Defense News reports France's armament agency awarded Airbus and Thales Alenia Space a contract worth more than one and a half billion euros, or about one point seven billion dollars, for two satellites in a system called IRIS. They will take extremely high resolution images day and night, and will replace three existing military observation satellites. The first is planned to become operational in 2033, with both in orbit around 2035.

United Launch Alliance had to delay Saturday's attempt to return its Vulcan rocket to flight. Spaceflight Now reports a power interruption at a nitrogen plant scrubbed Friday's attempt from Cape Canaveral, and a network communication issue between the ground and the rocket then pushed the launch to Sunday. The rocket is due to carry forty broadband satellites for Amazon's Leo constellation, the first of thirty-eight Vulcan launches Amazon has bought. Vulcan has not flown in nine months, after solid rocket booster nozzles failed on two of its first four missions, prompting the U S government to pause its use for national security launches.

Sierra Space is a step closer to flying its Dream Chaser spaceplane. SpaceNews reports the company received a reentry license from the Federal Aviation Administration that allows up to four landings a year at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. Sierra Space now plans a first flight in the spring of 2027.

SpaceX also picked up a military study contract. SpaceNews reports the Space Development Agency awarded the company nearly ten million dollars to study connecting its laser communications technology with missile warning and tracking satellites, as the Space Force works to link networks built around different optical standards.

In startup funding, Reflect Orbital announced seventy-three and a half million dollars in new funding, bringing its total to more than one hundred million. The Hawthorne, California company wants to redirect sunlight from space to Earth, for uses such as solar power after sunset. Investors include Sequoia Capital, Lux Capital and Salesforce Ventures. The company expects to launch its first demonstration mission in the second quarter of 2027. And SpaceNews reports French startup Gama, which pivoted from solar sails to large deployable solar arrays for spacecraft, raised fifteen million euros.
