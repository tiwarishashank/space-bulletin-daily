This is Space Bulletin Weekly with Nora. It's Saturday, October thirty-first.

NASA's administrator says the next Artemis mission cannot slip. In an interview with Florida Today, part of the USA Today Network, Jared Isaacman said Artemis Three "must fly" in 2027. The crew of four is set to launch next summer into low Earth orbit, where they will dock with Blue Origin's lander and briefly with a SpaceX Starship, to pave the way for a Moon landing on Artemis Four in 2028. Isaacman told the paper the mission should last fourteen to seventeen days. He said NASA needs to launch a big rocket at least once a year, adding, "Without that, you can't move on to Four." Asked what happens if China lands astronauts on the Moon first, he repeated what he said early in his tenure: "If the Chinese beat us back to the moon, I'll be fired." On Wednesday, NASA shared photos of the Artemis Three core stage and solid rocket boosters stacked upright inside the Vehicle Assembly Building at Kennedy Space Center. And SpaceNews reports Vietnam has signed the Artemis Accords, becoming the seventy-eighth country to do so.

Amazon's satellite internet business cleared a major hurdle this week. Reuters reports the Federal Communications Commission approved Amazon Leo's eleven point five billion dollar deal to acquire Globalstar. Amazon adds Globalstar's two dozen satellites and its mobile satellite spectrum, which the commission said will boost Amazon's entry into connecting phones directly to satellites. Globalstar powers emergency features on Apple's iPhone and Apple Watch, and Amazon says it has signed an agreement with Apple to keep providing those services. Reuters reports Amazon Leo has launched nearly four hundred satellites since April 2025, and plans to roll out commercial service by the end of this year.

Those satellites will soon have another ride. Ars Technica reports United Launch Alliance plans to return its Vulcan rocket to flight on November thirteenth, nine months after a solid rocket booster anomaly. The mission will carry forty Amazon Leo satellites, the first of thirty-eight Vulcan flights Amazon has booked.

SpaceX is getting ready for its next Starship test. Space dot com reports the company static-fired the Flight Fifteen upper stage on Monday and Tuesday, after a thirty-three engine test of the Super Heavy booster. SpaceX said the ship "performed a full duration, six-engine static fire." Elon Musk has said the flight is targeted for sometime in November, and SpaceX has said it will try to catch both the booster and the ship back at the launch tower.

Meanwhile, the workhorse Falcon Nine hit a milestone. Spaceflight Now reports Friday night's Starlink launch from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California was the seven hundredth Falcon Nine flight to date, and the booster landed on a droneship in the Pacific. It was SpaceX's eleventh and final orbital launch of October. Spaceflight Now notes the company's launch rate is slowing as it starts to wind down the Falcon program and turns its focus to Starship.

On the International Space Station, astronauts completed the one hundredth U S spacewalk from the outpost. Space dot com reports NASA's Anil Menon and Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk spent seven hours and eleven minutes outside on Thursday, installing and unrolling a new solar array. It was the first spacewalk by a Canadian astronaut since April 2019. The new arrays, used with the older ones, have increased the station's power supply by twenty to thirty percent. The pair is scheduled to go out again on November fifth.

In Earth observation, EarthDaily has won a three million dollar NASA task order. SpaceWatch Global reports NASA and the U S Geological Survey will test whether data from the company's satellites can meet the standards to work alongside Landsat, the government program that has tracked changes to Earth's land surface for more than five decades. EarthDaily says eight of its ten planned satellites are in orbit, and once all ten are working it expects to image about ninety-two percent of Earth's landmass daily, excluding Antarctica.

In startup funding, Colorado-based Rendezvous Robotics raised twenty million dollars in a seed round led by MaC Venture Capital and Toyota Ventures. The company announced the money on Wednesday and says it is building spacecraft that assemble themselves in orbit. Payload reports its first in-space demonstration is set for no earlier than April 2028, when seven spacecraft tiles are meant to join into one spacecraft, separate, and reassemble in a different shape.

And one broadband constellation is running late. SpaceNews reports delayed pathfinder satellites have pushed back global service for Telesat's Lightspeed network by three months, to around the middle of 2028. The Canadian operator says Arctic coverage for Canada's military remains on track for early 2028.
