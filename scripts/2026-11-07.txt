This is Space Bulletin Weekly with Nora. It's Saturday, November seventh.

A five-year effort to rewire the International Space Station's power supply is finished. Spaceflight Now reports NASA's Anil Menon and Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk unfurled the eighth and final new roll-out solar array on Thursday, during a spacewalk of six hours and fifty-nine minutes. About four and a half hours in, flight controllers saw the new array delivering only about half the expected current. Kutryk waited for orbital night, unplugged a power connector, inspected it and plugged it back in, and controllers then reported good currents. NASA says the eight arrays will raise the station's available power by up to thirty percent, supporting it through its planned retirement in 2030.

A long-serving NASA observatory is gone. Spaceflight Now reports the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory fell back into the atmosphere on Wednesday over North Sumatra in western Indonesia, ending nearly twenty-two years in orbit. Swift was designed to operate for two years. Over its career it observed more than two thousand gamma-ray bursts and studied more than fourteen hundred supernovae. Solar activity swelled the upper atmosphere and dragged it down faster than expected, and a thirty million dollar robotic rescue by Katalyst Space was called off in August after its tug lost control of its orientation. NASA said some components may have survived reentry.

In deep space, Europe's Hera mission has its first close look at its target. The European Space Agency says Hera's framing camera photographed the Didymos asteroid from sixty kilometers away, showing the side the NASA spacecraft DART never saw before it slammed into the smaller moonlet Dimorphos in 2022. Principal investigator Patrick Michel said, "The first thing we said was wow," describing boulders, craters and patterns of grooves or ridges. ESA says detailed images of both asteroids will be released on November twenty-fifth.

SpaceX now has a date for its most ambitious Starship test. Teslarati, citing Ars Technica, reports SpaceX vice president of launch Kiko Dontchev said on stage at the UP Summit on Thursday that Flight Fifteen is targeted for Friday, November thirteenth. The mission is expected to include the first attempt to catch the ship itself with the launch tower, alongside the booster. SpaceX has not posted an official mission page or launch time.

Meanwhile, Falcon Nine flew a classified mission. Space dot com reports a rocket lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California on Friday evening on the U S S F four thirty-two mission for the U S Space Force. Neither SpaceX nor the Space Force said much about the payload, and Spaceflight Now says it is thought to carry satellites based on SpaceX's Starshield platform. Space dot com counts it as SpaceX's one hundred twenty-fifth launch of the year.

Blue Origin used the same UP Summit to unveil new hardware. Spaceflight Now reports chief executive Dave Limp announced a third stage for New Glenn, called Glenn Stage Three, powered by the BE-Seven engine from the company's lunar landers. Blue Origin says it can deliver up to fourteen metric tons directly to geostationary orbit, and that a government mission to cislunar space is already booked for 2028. The company also revealed Northstar, a new first-stage engine rated at seven hundred fifteen thousand pounds of thrust, compared with six hundred forty thousand for the BE-Four. New Glenn is still working toward its first flight since a pad explosion in late May.

In a major deal, satellite operator AST SpaceMobile plans to build its own rocket. Spaceflight Now reports the company signed a one point three billion dollar agreement with Firefly Aerospace for engines and a license to manufacture them. The rocket, called Blacklift, is meant to launch five of AST's BlueBird satellites at a time, starting in 2028 if all goes to plan. Firefly will deliver twenty-seven Miranda engines and nine Vira engines for the first three rockets. Neither engine has flown to orbit yet.

In funding news, Austin-based CesiumAstro closed a seven hundred thirty million dollar Series D round led by Janus Henderson, valuing the company at more than four and a half billion dollars. Payload reports the total includes one hundred million dollars in debt from the Export-Import Bank of the United States and JPMorgan. The company also unveiled plans for Synchronicity, a constellation of seven hundred thirty-seven satellites for commercial and defense customers.

And in Earth observation, Europe's newest weather satellite has opened its eyes. ESA says the second Meteosat Third Generation imager has delivered its first images, including fog over central Europe, storm clouds over the Mediterranean and a wildfire smoke plume in Portugal. Once operational in the second quarter of 2027, it will scan Europe and North Africa every two and a half minutes.
