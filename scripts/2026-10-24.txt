This is Space Bulletin Weekly with Nora. It's Saturday, October twenty-fourth.

NASA has laid out its most detailed plan yet for getting astronauts back to the Moon. At a news conference at Kennedy Space Center on Friday, Administrator Jared Isaacman said the agency is still targeting Artemis Three for the summer of 2027, and Artemis Four, the first crewed landing, for 2028. NASA says Artemis Three will send NASA astronauts Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas, along with European Space Agency astronaut Luca Parmitano, into low Earth orbit aboard Orion for a planned fourteen-day mission. Space dot com reports the crew would dock with Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander on the third day and spend two days testing it, then meet a SpaceX Starship on day eight for a docking demonstration of about two hours. Ars Technica reports NASA is also working with United Launch Alliance on an extended version of the Centaur Five upper stage, so Orion can reach a low orbit around the Moon. Isaacman called it "an achievable path."

That briefing came a day after a warning from NASA's own safety advisers. SpacePolicyOnline reports the Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel will recommend that NASA re-examine the mission objectives, and potentially the architecture, for Artemis Four. Panel member Charlie Precourt said they had been told twenty-two launches would be needed to fuel the Starship lander for the trip. Elon Musk said on X that figure was wrong and that it would be six to eight. A NASA official said this week that SpaceX plans to demonstrate transferring super-cold propellant in orbit in early 2027. The panel also warned about future access to low Earth orbit, as SpaceX plans to phase out its Falcon rockets and Dragon capsules once Starship is reliable. It said Boeing's next uncrewed Starliner test is no earlier than mid-January.

SpaceX reported its third-quarter results on Tuesday. In a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, the company said revenue rose one hundred sixty-one percent from a year earlier, to fourteen point six billion dollars, with net income of four point four billion. Reuters reports revenue beat analysts' estimates, driven by a surge in the company's artificial intelligence business. The Associated Press reports the A I business became SpaceX's largest by revenue, although Starlink remains its biggest profit generator. CNBC reports Starlink subscribers grew to thirteen point six million, and connectivity revenue rose sixty-nine percent, to about five point two five billion dollars. SpaceX also said it flew its first revenue-generating Starship mission, delivering twenty-six Starlink satellites.

In startup funding, satellite navigation company Xona Space Systems raised two hundred fifty million dollars. SpaceNews reports the Series D round was led by Future Ventures and values the company at two point four five billion dollars. Xona says six of its Pulsar satellites launched this month, and four of them will support a beta service in early 2027. The company is building a low-orbit navigation network it says will be more precise and harder to jam than GPS.

The Space Force has signed its first operational contracts to call on commercial satellite operators during a crisis. SpaceNews reports six companies were selected under the Commercial Augmentation Space Reserve. Breaking Defense names them as EOS Space Systems, NorthStar Earth and Space, InTrack Radar, Anduril, Slingshot Aerospace and LeoLabs, in short-term agreements worth a combined one point three seven million dollars for tracking objects in orbit.

In Earth observation, weather-data company PlanetiQ now has five satellites in orbit. Via Satellite reports the company's GNOMES-Eleven satellite flew on SpaceX's Bandwagon mission this week, its second launch this month. Chief executive Ira Scharf says the network is approaching nearly twenty thousand atmospheric profiles a day, used to measure temperature, pressure and humidity, and the company is building toward twenty or more satellites.

China has started a new remote sensing network aimed at low latitudes. SpaceNews reports a commercial Ceres-One rocket launched from a ship in the Yellow Sea on Wednesday, carrying the first satellite of the Wuyang constellation. China's CGTN says the satellite is in an orbit about five hundred forty-one kilometers up, and will support farm monitoring, marine observation, and flood and typhoon response across southern China.

And Ukraine is exploring a satellite network of its own. SpaceNews reports the move follows restrictions on SpaceX services that disrupted Ukrainian military operations. Finnish companies Iceye and Nokia say they signed a non-binding letter of intent with Ukraine's Ministry of Defence for a secure communications network that Kyiv would control, with the first satellites expected in 2028.
